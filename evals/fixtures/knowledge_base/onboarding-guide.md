# Onboarding Guide

This guide describes what happens in a new employee's first two weeks and
what a manager is responsible for arranging before the new hire's start
date. It complements the Employee Handbook, which describes ongoing
policies rather than the specific sequence of onboarding steps.

People Operations owns the overall onboarding process, but the hiring
manager is responsible for the team-specific parts: introductions, initial
project assignment, and making sure the new hire has someone to ask
questions of on their first day.

## Before day one

The hiring manager submits an equipment request through the IT request form
at least one week before the start date so a laptop is ready on arrival.
People Operations sends the new hire their offer paperwork, benefits
enrollment information, and a first-day agenda before their start date.

Accounts for email, chat, source control, and the document knowledge base
are provisioned automatically the morning of the start date based on the
role recorded in the HR system; a role that needs additional access beyond
the default, such as production database access, still goes through the
normal access request form once the new hire has started, not before.

## First day

The first day includes a company-wide orientation covering the Employee
Handbook, the Security Policy basics, and how benefits enrollment works.
New hires are asked to acknowledge that they have read the Employee
Handbook and the Security Policy by the end of their first week, not
necessarily the first day.

The hiring manager or a designated buddy meets the new hire in person or on
a video call on the first day to walk through team-specific tools, current
projects, and who's who on the team. This is separate from the
company-wide orientation and should not be skipped even for an experienced
hire.

## First week

New hires complete required training during their first week, including
the security awareness training referenced in the Security Policy and any
role-specific compliance training. Engineering new hires are expected to
ship a small, low-risk change in their first week, both to exercise the
development and deployment tooling and to build early confidence; managers
select this first task deliberately rather than assigning whatever is at
the top of the backlog.

Remote and hybrid new hires follow the same first-week structure as
in-office hires; the Remote Work Policy applies to a new hire from day one,
not after some initial in-office period.

## First thirty days

By the end of the first month, the new hire and their manager should have
had at least one check-in specifically about how onboarding is going,
separate from any regular one-on-one, so that friction in the process gets
caught while it is still easy to fix. New hires are not expected to be
fully independent by day thirty; the thirty-day check-in is about the
onboarding experience, not a performance evaluation.

## First ninety days

The first formal goal-setting conversation happens around the ninety-day
mark, aligning the new hire with the twice-yearly performance cycle
described in the Employee Handbook. This is also typically when access
needs become clearer as the new hire's actual day-to-day work solidifies,
and any standing access beyond the default should be requested at that
point rather than accumulated informally.

## Common onboarding issues

The most common onboarding problem is a delayed equipment order, usually
because the request was submitted less than a week before the start date;
managers are reminded of this deadline in the hiring confirmation email.
The second most common issue is a new hire not being added to the right
team channels and calendars, which People Operations checks against the HR
system record but which is ultimately the hiring manager's responsibility
to confirm on day one.

## Manager checklist

Before a new hire's start date, the hiring manager confirms the equipment
request has been submitted, drafts a rough first-two-weeks plan, and
identifies a buddy if the manager will not personally be available for
most of the first day. This checklist is tracked in the same HR system
that drives account provisioning, so People Operations can see at a glance
which upcoming start dates are missing a piece of manager preparation.

## Onboarding for different roles

Engineering onboarding, as described above, emphasizes shipping a small
change in the first week. Non-engineering roles follow an analogous
principle of an early, low-risk, real piece of work rather than a purely
observational first week, adapted to the role: a sales new hire might
shadow a call and then run a small piece of one themselves, and a support
new hire might handle a simple ticket under supervision before handling
one independently.

Contractor and vendor onboarding is lighter weight than employee
onboarding and is covered by the Vendor Access Policy rather than this
guide, since a contractor typically does not go through the full benefits
and HR system setup an employee does.

## Returning employees

An employee who leaves and is later rehired follows a modified onboarding
process that skips redundant orientation content but still requires a
fresh acknowledgment of the current Employee Handbook and Security Policy,
since both may have changed since the employee's departure. Account
provisioning for a returning employee still goes through the standard
process rather than attempting to restore the employee's previous access,
which is deliberately not preserved across a departure.

## Feedback on the onboarding process

New hires are asked to complete a short survey at the thirty-day mark and
again at ninety days, feeding into the check-ins described above. People
Operations aggregates this feedback quarterly to identify systemic issues
with onboarding, separate from any individual new hire's specific
experience, and shares a summary with Engineering and other department
leads so the process can improve over time rather than staying static.

## Internal transfer onboarding

An employee moving to a new team internally, as described in the Employee
Handbook's learning and development section, goes through a lighter
version of this onboarding guide focused on the new team's specific tools
and context, since company-wide orientation and policy acknowledgment do
not need to be repeated. The new manager is still expected to have a
first-day conversation and to identify a buddy, exactly as with an
external new hire.
